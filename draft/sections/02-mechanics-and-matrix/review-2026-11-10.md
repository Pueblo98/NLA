### 2.2 Triangle forces and assembly

The formula $K_e=tS_eB_e^TDB_e$ and the equal endpoint loads for **constant** traction are correct. The problem is the missing explanation between the formulas. Six displacements means three vertices times two directions, not six vertices. $B_e$ converts those displacements into strain, $D$ converts strain into stress, and the remaining factors convert that stress into the triangle's contribution to nodal force. These operations follow the strain vector already defined in 2.1. Vertex coordinates determine $B_e$; it is not a further unknown to solve for.

Assembly means adding contributions into the rows and columns for the same global displacement components. Adjacent triangles use the same displacement at a shared vertex, while their internal-force contributions add. We do not average their stiffness matrices or duplicate the shared displacement unknowns. The actual index mapping and summation in `fem/elasticity.py:assemble` agree with this interpretation.

**Suggested replacement for 2.2, at roughly the posted length:**

> Each triangle has three vertices, each with a horizontal and vertical displacement. These six values form $d_e$. Linear interpolation gives a constant strain $B_ed_e$, where the matrix $B_e$ is calculated from the vertex coordinates. Combining the material law with the work balance introduced in Section 1 gives
>
> $$K_e=tS_eB_e^TDB_e.$$
>
> Here $S_e$ is the triangle's area. This $6\times6$ matrix converts its vertex displacements into internal nodal forces [4]. Assembly adds its entries into the rows and columns of $K$ for those vertices, ordered $(u_{x,0},u_{y,0},u_{x,1},u_{y,1},\ldots)$. Contributions from triangles meeting at a node add together. Nodes sharing no triangle have no direct coupling, making $K$ sparse. The mesh follows the object's boundaries and leaves holes empty [3].
>
> External loads form $f$. A uniform boundary force per unit length $q$ gives each endpoint of an edge of length $\ell$ the force $\ell q/2$ [4]. If specified as traction $\bar\tau$ in N/m², then $q=t\bar\tau$ in N/m. Thus $f$ is in newtons and $K$ in N/m. Refinement preserves the physical load, rather than the force per node. Expanded formulas are in the [companion note](derivation-notes.md).

Retain the existing course-routine footnote at the assembly paragraph. Put the `fem/elasticity.py:assemble` path in that footnote or the supporting note; it interrupts the paper's explanation in its current position. Retain the two existing citations [3] and [4], using their BibTeX keys in LaTeX. No new bibliography entry is needed.

The endpoint split is specifically for an edge with uniform traction and linear interpolation. It is not a rule for arbitrary varying loads. For intuition, splitting a uniformly loaded edge into two equal edges halves each edge's endpoint contribution; the shared middle node receives contributions from both edges, and the total applied force stays the same. This explanation belongs in the review/notebook, not as another paragraph in the paper. The stiffness construction and constant-traction endpoint split are supported by the [University of Alberta triangular-element derivation](https://engcourses-uofa.ca/books/introduction-to-solid-mechanics/finite-element-analysis/two-dimensional-solid-elements/triangular-elements/).
